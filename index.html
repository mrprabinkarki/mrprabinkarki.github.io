About

I'm a PhD candidate in Physics, with a focus on biophysics, at the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, working in the lab of Prof. Marius Schmidt. I have over three years of hands-on experience determining macromolecular structures with X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM, from protein expression and purification through data processing to model building.

My PhD research centres on bacteriophytochromes: how they change shape when they absorb light, and how they pass that signal on to their partner proteins. I lead the project on the phytochrome–response regulator complex.

Before UWM, I earned my M.Sc. and B.Sc. in Physics at Tribhuvan University in Nepal. I'm looking for postdoctoral positions in structural biology and drug discovery, and I'm open to relocating.

Research

How does a protein turn a photon into a biochemical signal? I approach this question from the structure side.

Phytochrome signaling complexes

Cryo-EM structures of a full-length phytochrome histidine kinase bound to its response regulator. The regulator binds as a dimer, and light loosens the complex, so light controls signaling both through kinase activity and through reversible binding.

Time-resolved cryo-EM

Illuminating particles on the grid and vitrifying them 10 ms later with the Spotiton technique. This captured a phytochrome Pr/Pfr heterodimer and revealed a 180° rotation of the kinase domain on light activation.

Time-resolved crystallography

Serial crystallography at synchrotrons and X-ray free-electron lasers to follow structural changes in photoreceptors and enzymes, in collaboration with groups in the US and Europe.

Pr/Pr homodimer · resting state

Both protomers are in the red-absorbing Pr state. The coiled-coil helices hold the histidine kinase in its resting orientation, so it can phosphorylate itself and pass the phosphate on to the bound response regulator.
